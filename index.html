🛰 ISS pass predictor

next visible overhead passes
How these passes are worked out
  1. TLE propagation. The station's orbital elements come from live Celestrak two-line element sets, propagated forward with Skyfield (SGP4) to predict exactly where ISS will be — no orbit model is maintained in this page.
  2. Your latitude & longitude fix the observer point. A pass begins when the station rises more than 10° above your local horizon, peaks at max elevation, and sets again a few minutes later. Press 📍 use my location or type coordinates.
  3. Solar illumination decides whether you could actually see it: ISS is only visible when it is sunlit while your sky is dark. The sun angle at the station and at the observer are compared per pass, so an overhead pass at noon is correctly labelled a daylight pass and hidden by visible only.
Times are UTC. A 10° pass lasts only a few minutes, so stand outside a couple of minutes early. Prediction is refreshed live and cached for 30 minutes per location.

📚 Deep dive · why anyone tracks these

planetary defense in one screen

A near-Earth object is the only natural hazard humanity has ever been able to do something about in advance. Earthquakes, hurricanes and volcanoes arrive on their own schedule; an asteroid keeps an appointment that can be read decades ahead. That asymmetry is the whole reason the tracking exists — not curiosity about rocks, but the fact that a collision is, uniquely, avoidable if it is found early enough.

The 2022 DART mission closed the loop: a spacecraft was flown into the moonlet Dimorphos and measurably shortened its orbit around Didymos. Deflection moved from theory to demonstrated engineering. What DART did not demonstrate is the hard part — you need years of warning to nudge something far enough. A kinetic impactor changes a trajectory by roughly the width of the object itself; the miss distance accumulates only with time.

So the bottleneck is the catalogue, not the rocket. Congress asked NASA in 1998 to find 90% of the objects 1 km and larger; that population is now essentially accounted for. The current goal — 140 m and up — is not close to complete, and Chelyabinsk in 2013 is the reason that matters: a ~20 m object, arriving with no warning at all, airburst with about 30 times the energy of the Hiroshima bomb and injured roughly 1,500 people through shattered glass. Objects that size sit below most survey detection thresholds, and they do not need to reach the ground to cause harm.

Two numbers decide whether a newly found object deserves attention, and neither is size:

  • How close it can come. The orbit is computed forward for centuries and squashed into a single figure — the MOID, the minimum orbit intersection distance. If that never gets small, no amount of mass makes an object dangerous.
  • How uncertain the orbit is. A brand-new object has an arc of a few days, and the resulting probability cone is enormous. Most PHA designations are withdrawn within weeks as the arc lengthens. The 2004 object Apophis climbed to level 4 on the Torino Scale — the highest score any object has reached — before a precovery image from 2004 settled its orbit and ruled out the 2029 impact entirely.

That pattern — alarm, then resolution — is the normal outcome, and it is a sign the system works rather than a sign of false alarm. A tracking programme that never changes its mind is one that is not looking hard enough.

The table on this page is not a risk assessment. It is a list of objects passing reasonably close; the largest is usually a few hundred metres with a zero percent chance of impact for the next century. NASA's impact-risk analysis lives at the Center for Near-Earth Object Studies, and a PHA flag there means "worth watching", never "incoming".

🔤 The vocabulary

what the words actually mean
Asteroid
A rocky or metallic body orbiting the Sun, smaller than a planet and larger than about a metre, left over from the solar system's formation. Most live in the main belt between Mars and Jupiter and will never come near us.
NEO
near-Earth object
Any asteroid or comet whose orbit brings it within about 1.3 AU of the Sun — close enough that it can, over millennia, reach Earth's neighbourhood. Roughly 35,000 are known, and the count rises weekly. "Near" is astronomical: most never come closer than the Moon.
PHA
potentially hazardous asteroid
A formal, mechanical definition, not a judgement call: the orbit must come within 0.05 AU (about 19.5 lunar distances) of Earth and the object must be H ≤ 22, which corresponds to roughly 140 m across. Both conditions are required. About 5% of NEOs qualify, which means a PHA is a common, unremarkable classification.
Meteoroid
A much smaller body in space — the IAU draws no sharp boundary, and in practice the term covers fragments from about 30 µm up to a metre. Below that it is interplanetary dust; above it, an asteroid.
Meteor
The light, not the object. The streak is a trail of ionised air around something burning up at 70–100 km altitude. A meteor is an event, not a rock.
Meteorite
Whatever reaches the ground. Most metre-scale objects break apart in the atmosphere and never produce one.
Bolide
A meteor bright enough to explode, usually fragmenting in the air. Chelyabinsk was a bolide; seismic and infrasound stations detected it worldwide though no meteorite of significant size was recovered.
AU
Astronomical unit — the mean Earth–Sun distance, about 149.6 million km. The unit every distance on this page is measured in.
Lunar distance
Distance to the Moon, about 384,400 km. The asteroid table uses it because it makes "how close is close" legible: 1 LD is the Moon's orbit, 20 LD is a comfortable miss.

⚠ The Torino Scale

0–10 · impact hazard

Designed for communicating with the public: a single integer, 0 to 10, combining the probability of impact with the kinetic energy of the object. Colour-coded so a glance carries the meaning. It is deliberately conservative — anything with a nonzero but tiny probability lands at 0.

LevelMeaning
0No hazard. Either the chance is zero, or so close to it that it falls below the background rate of random impacts.
1Normal. A routine discovery that will almost certainly be downgraded to 0 as the orbit is refined. No public concern.
2Merits attention by astronomers — a close but not unusual encounter. Still not a public concern.
3 · 4Close encounter, merits attention. At 3 the chance of localised destruction exceeds 1%; at 4 it is global. Alerting the public is warranted at 4.
5 · 6 · 7Threatening. A significant chance of regional destruction at 5–6, or unprecedented at 7. These levels are reserved for a real, unresolved threat.
8 · 9 · 10Certain collision. 8 causes localised, 9 regional and 10 global destruction on impact. Empty for the entire history of the scale.

Apophis holds the record at level 4, reached in December 2004 and withdrawn days later once archival observations pinned its orbit. Asteroids currently on the Sentry risk list sit at 0.

📐 The Palermo Scale

logarithmic · technical

Where Torino is for the public, Palermo is for the people doing the work. It asks a sharper question than "how likely is an impact": how much more likely is this object to hit us than the background rate of objects its size?

The value is the logarithm of the ratio of the object's impact risk to the average risk from the whole population of similar objects over the same period. Being logarithmic, the numbers are small but steep:

ValueReading
below −2No cause for concern. The object is less threatening than the everyday background. Almost everything ever discovered sits here.
−2 to 0Merits careful monitoring. Between 100× and 1× the background risk. Worth follow-up observation, not alarm.
0 to +2Concerning. Up to 100× the background rate. This range has been reached only a handful of times.
above +2Threat. More than 100× background. No object has ever scored here.

Because it is a continuous scale, Palermo can separate two objects that both read "Torino 1" but differ by orders of magnitude in actual risk — which is exactly the resolution a survey programme needs and a colour code cannot give.

Both scales share one property worth holding onto: they change. A score is a statement about the current orbit fit, not about the object. Better data revises it, and nearly always downward.

🪨 Asteroid classes

C · S · M and the rest

Class is inferred from colour and albedo — how much sunlight the surface reflects and at which wavelengths — because almost no NEO is resolved as more than a point of light. The classes map closely onto composition, and composition decides both what a body is worth mining and how hard it is to see.

TypeShareMake-up and behaviour
C · carbonaceous~75%The most common asteroid type, and the darkest — albedo around 0.03–0.09, reflecting almost nothing. Carbon-rich, chemically primitive, water-bearing clay minerals. Essentially unaltered since the solar system formed, which is why they are prime targets for sample return — Ryugu (Hayabusa2) and Bennu (OSIRIS-REx) are both carbonaceous.
S · silicaceous~17%Stony, moderate albedo (0.10–0.22). Silicates mixed with iron and nickel, altered by heat and water early on. Their meteorites — ordinary chondrites — are the most common type found on Earth, which is a selection effect: S-types dominate the inner belt, close to the resonances that fling material inward.
M · metallic~8% of beltNickel–iron, relatively bright (0.10–0.20). Thought to be the exposed cores of differentiated bodies shattered by collisions. The source of iron meteorites, and the reason 16 Psyche is being visited — a metal core that can be studied without digging a planet open.
V · vestoidsmallBasaltic rock from the crust of 4 Vesta, stripped off by an ancient impact. The HED meteorites come from here.
P / DouterVery dark and reddish, concentrated in the outer belt and among Jupiter's Trojans. Probably ice-rich and organic; the most likely parent material of comets.

There is a real consequence hiding in that albedo spread, and it applies to the table above. A C-type reflects a tenth of what an S-type does, so at the same size it appears much fainter — and a faint object is found later, tracked less precisely, and easier to miss entirely. Dark asteroids are both the most common and the hardest to catalogue.

🔬 How the numbers above are actually derived

an estimate, not a measurement

Diameter. Almost no object passing Earth is large enough to resolve as a disk, even for Hubble. NASA publishes an estimated diameter computed from the object's absolute magnitude H — how bright it would look 1 AU from the Sun and 1 AU from Earth — combined with an assumed albedo. The range shown in the table is that assumption expressed as a bracket, and it is wide on purpose.

This means the column is an inference dressed as a number. A 100 m carbonaceous asteroid with a dark surface and a 100 m silicaceous one with a bright surface do not look alike from Earth, and the estimate resolves the ambiguity by guessing. Anything known only from a short arc of observation can be off by a factor of two or more.

Close approach and velocity. Both are relative to Earth at the moment of the encounter, and the velocity is dominated by the geometry of the intersection rather than the object's own speed around the Sun. Two asteroids on similar orbits can differ by tens of km/s purely because one meets Earth head-on and the other catches up from behind — which is also why the second kind is far easier to launch a mission to.

Brightness (H). Smaller H means brighter. Counter-intuitively, a low H means a larger object, because it reflects more light. H is the input to the size estimate, which is why the two columns move together rather than independently.

Read the diameter column as an order of magnitude, not a figure. It is the single most-flagged estimate on this page, and the API says so itself by returning a range.

📚 Deep dive · reading the Sun

flares, CMEs, and what the numbers above mean

A solar flare is a sudden release of magnetic energy in the corona — the Sun's outer atmosphere — dumping energy into plasma and accelerating particles. What reaches Earth first is radiation, travelling at light speed, which is why a flare is felt about eight minutes after it happens. Nothing can be forecast to the minute; what is predictable is that a sunspot complex with tangled, opposite-polarity fields is more likely to produce one.

Flares are graded by the X-ray flux GOES measures in the 1–8 Å band, in watts per square metre. The classes step by decades, so each letter is ten times the one before it, and the number subdivides that decade — an M5 is five tenths of the way from M1 to X1.

ClassFlux (W/m²)What it typically does
A B< 10⁻⁶Background. Constant, unremarkable, no terrestrial effect.
C10⁻⁶ – 10⁻⁵Small. Faint HF radio degradation on the sunlit side at high latitudes, usually unnoticed.
M10⁻⁵ – 10⁻⁴Medium. Brief radio blackouts (minutes) on the sunlit side, polar HF absorption, and a good chance of an accompanying CME. M-class is the everyday working level during solar maximum.
X≥ 10⁻⁴Large. Wide, long HF blackouts; often a fast CME and a solar radiation storm behind it. The scale is open-ended — the 2003 Halloween events reached an estimated X45.

The class alone does not tell you whether Earth is affected. A flare on the far limb, aimed away, is scientifically interesting and operationally irrelevant.

🌪 Coronal mass ejections

not the same thing as a flare

This is the most common misunderstanding in space weather, and it matters. A flare is radiation — it arrives in eight minutes and is over. A coronal mass ejection is matter: on the order of a billion tonnes of magnetised plasma thrown off the corona, expanding at anywhere from 250 to 3,000 km/s. Where a flare gives warning measured in minutes, a CME gives 15 hours to three days — the difference between an event you can only observe and one you can prepare for.

The two are related but not paired. Many flares launch no CME; many CMEs come from filament eruptions that produce no flare at all. So a big X-class flare with nothing behind it is a much smaller event than the class suggests.

What decides whether a CME does anything at Earth is its magnetic orientation. Earth's field points north on the dayside facing the Sun. A CME whose own field points south couples to it — the two fields reconnect, opening the magnetosphere to the solar wind and pouring energy into the ring current and the auroral zones. A CME with a northward field largely slides past. That single component is what the IMF Bz tile above is reporting, and it is why a middling CME with the right orientation can outperform a monster with the wrong one.

Aurora follows a southward turning by roughly 30–60 minutes, not instantly — the delay is the time for the magnetosphere to load and then release.

📏 The NOAA scales

G · R · S

Three separate phenomena, three separate 1-to-5 scales. They are routinely conflated, and the letter rather than the number is what carries the meaning.

ScaleDriver1 → 5
G geomagneticKpStorm: G1 Kp 5 → G5 Kp 9. Grid voltage irregularities, satellite drag and orientation problems, degraded GNSS. The scale that drives aurora.
R radio blackoutX-rayHF fade on the sunlit side: R1 M1 → R5 X20+. Arrives with the flare, lasts minutes to hours, and is the fastest effect of any solar event.
S radiation stormprotonsEnergetic protons: S1 → S5. Solar panels degrade, single-event upsets hit electronics, and polar flight crews face elevated dose — which is why some transpolar routes are rerouted during a strong S event.

NOAA uses geomagnetic latitude, not the geographic latitude on a normal map, and the two differ substantially — in North America the magnetic pole pulls the auroral zone noticeably south of where longitude lines suggest. This is the single biggest reason people at "the wrong latitude" see aurora anyway, and people at "the right latitude" miss it.

🟢 Auroras and the Kp index

why the oval grows, and when you can see it

Kp is a 3-hour planetary index running from 0 to 9, in thirds. It is not measured by a satellite — it is derived from magnetometer stations at mid-latitudes, each producing a local K index, which are then normalised and averaged. That is why it is planetary and why it is coarse: it compresses three hours and half the globe into one number. It is also quasi-logarithmic, so the jump from Kp 7 to Kp 8 is a much larger physical change than 3 to 4.

The auroral oval is permanent — a ring of glowing atmosphere around each magnetic pole, present every night of the year. What changes is its size. Solar wind energy entering the magnetosphere drives currents that expand the oval equatorward, and Kp is the accepted shorthand for how far it has come. A quiet Kp 2 keeps the oval over the Arctic; at Kp 9 its equatorward edge can reach the latitude of the Mediterranean.

KpScaleOval edge (geomagnetic)
0 – 4—Quiet. Aurora confined to 65° + — northern Scandinavia, Alaska, northern Canada.
5G1≈ 60°. Overhead in Iceland, northern Scotland, the Baltic.
6G2≈ 55°. Visible on the horizon from northern England, the northern US border states.
7G3≈ 50°. Overhead across the UK and northern Germany; on the horizon from Paris.
8G4≈ 45°. The May 2024 G5 storm was seen from Mexico and the Mediterranean — a once-in-decades event.
9G5≈ 40°. Aurora near the tropics; the 2003 Halloween storms turned the sky red over Florida.

Kp is not a visibility forecast. A high Kp with the oval on the far side of the planet from you shows you nothing, and "on the horizon" aurora is a faint red glow easily lost to light pollution, moonlight or haze. Dark skies and a clear view toward the magnetic pole matter more than the number for most observers — which is why the OVATION overlay on the Natural Events map is the more useful guide: it plots where the oval actually is right now, rather than how large it might be.

The colour is altitude and chemistry, not temperature. Solar particles excite oxygen and nitrogen at different heights: green (557.7 nm) from atomic oxygen at 100–150 km, red (630 nm) from oxygen far higher at 200–400 km, and violet-blue fringes from molecular nitrogen. The most common aurora is green, and the red seen from low latitudes in a great storm is the high-altitude oxygen layer being lit up overhead.

The phenomenon

☀ sunlight still strikes 100–300 km observer below in twilight → dark sky

A space jellyfish — also called the twilight phenomenon — is a rocket's exhaust plume seen lit from below the horizon. It needs two things to be true at once, in different places:

  • The observer is already in twilight, so the sky above them is dark enough to see detail; and
  • the rocket, 100–300 km up, is still in direct sunlight, because at that altitude it is far enough over the curve of the Earth to catch the Sun while the ground below is in shadow.

In near-vacuum the exhaust does not stay a tight tail. It expands freely, ballooning to tens of kilometres across, and the thin residuals glow as the gas is excited by sunlight. The result reads as a bell-shaped head with trailing tentacles — hence the name. The expanding shock front can also light up as a faint halo or ring around the head, which is why good photographs often look like a ringed nebula rather than a plume.

Nothing about the rocket is special; the geometry is. The same launch at noon shows an ordinary white plume and the same launch at 2 a.m. shows nothing at all. There is no engine type, fuel or vehicle that produces a jellyfish — it is entirely a question of where the Sun is.

Where it is visible from. Two windows in every day:

  • Roughly 20–60 minutes after local sunset, or 20–60 minutes before local sunrise. This is when the ground is dark but the atmosphere at 200 km is still sunlit. It is the only time the effect exists — judged here by the Sun's elevation at the pad, which is why the bands in the outlook are twilight only.
  • 50–300 km from the pad gives the classic overhead view, and it has been seen from well over 500 km away during the high-altitude phase. "Too far" is less limiting than people assume — line of sight to the horizon matters more than distance, which is why the outlook reports the bearing to look along and the plume's height above your own horizon rather than a simple yes.
  • The reach is direction-dependent, not a radius. At one instant the Sun's elevation is fixed, so the ground that is dark enough is the half of the map away from the Sun, and the day side of the pad can be lit within a few hundred kilometres. Every row therefore quotes a sighting band measured direction by direction — often something like “1,200 km in most directions, but only 300 km to the east” — and the side named as short is the side the Sun is rising or setting on, which flips between dawn and dusk at the same pad.
  • Low horizon, clear sky, no moon. Light pollution fogs the low sky exactly where the phenomenon sits, so a dark site with an unobstructed view in the direction of the pad is worth more than proximity.

It is not the same as a noctilucent cloud (ice crystals at 80 km, polar summer only) or a rocket’s re-entry burn. Nor is it a failure or an anomaly — it is a lighting condition, and it appears most often on perfectly nominal launches.
